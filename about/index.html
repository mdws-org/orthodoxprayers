About

A prayer book for the browser. For the morning: the whole morning rule, the morning prayer of St. Philaret of Moscow, the prayer at daybreak of Elder Sophrony and the commemoration of the living and the dead. Through the day: the Hours, the Typika and the prayers at the table. For the evening: Compline, the whole evening rule, the evening prayer of St. Macarius the Great and the prayer at bedtime of St. John of Damascus. Before Holy Communion: the Three Canons, the canon of preparation and the prayers before Communion, with the thanksgiving after Communion. And the whole Psalter, in twenty kathismata.

The prayers the services name only by their first words (O Heavenly King, Holy God, Our Father and the rest) are given in full on the Common Prayers page.

There is no account to make and nothing to install. The first visit saves the whole book to the device, so every page opens afterwards with the signal off. Added to a phone home screen, it opens like an app.

Colophon

The pages are set the way the printed prayer books set them: black and red, an ornament at the head and a red initial two lines deep opening the first prayer on each page of prayers, with the rest of its first word in small caps. Each initial stands as close to the lines beside it as in Prayers for Morning, Day and Night (St. Tikhon’s Monastery Press, 2019).

The type is EB Garamond. The site justifies the text a paragraph at a time, so a phone’s narrow column keeps even word spaces. The hyphenation patterns come from the justif library, under the MIT License.

The ornament bars at the head of each page come from the Orthodox Illustration Project. They are vector drawings, so they stay sharp at any size and invert cleanly for the night palette. There are six. The date picks the bar, not chance, and each page has its own: everyone who opens a given page on a given day sees the same ornament, and it changes at midnight.

The small cross at the head and foot of each page comes from the Ponomar Project’s font for Church Slavonic, used under the SIL Open Font License; it is the cross that heads each section in the prayer books of St. Tikhon’s Monastery Press. The leaf that stands before and after a passage said only in some cases is EB Garamond’s own.

The Psalter follows the twenty kathismata of the Slavonic use. The Psalter page points to the kathisma appointed for the day of the month: the first on the first, through the twentieth on the twentieth, then the first again on the twenty-first. Each kathisma closes with the troparia appointed after it, Lord, have mercy forty times, and the prayer proper to that kathisma.

Some terms in the rubrics and headings (irmos, troparion, kathisma and others) carry a short note; tap the term to read it. The notes are the site’s own words, checked against the 1982 prayer book, oca.org and other Orthodox sources.

Day and Night follow your system setting until you pick one, and the site remembers. In either theme a page prints black and red on white, without the running head, the buttons or the footer.

There are no analytics, and nothing here is fetched from another server. Every font, script and image comes from this domain.

Set and maintained by Benjamin Meadows. Corrections are welcome at thebenmeadows.com.

Sources

The evening rule, Compline, the Three Canons and the Communion prayers are set word for word from Orthodox Daily Prayers (St. Tikhon’s Seminary Press, 1982), as Project Gutenberg has it, except for a few corrections, each one recorded and checked. The morning rule, the Commemoration, the evening prayer of St. Macarius, the prayer at bedtime and the prayers at the table are set from the same book.

The Typika is set word for word from a printed Reader’s Service booklet, undated and published without a copyright notice before 1978. Its Beatitudes and Psalm 33 follow the Revised Standard Version, which is still in copyright, so those two passages are set instead from Isabel Hapgood’s Service Book of the Holy Orthodox-Catholic Apostolic Church (1906).

The Psalter is adapted from The Psalter of St. Seraphim Orthodox Cathedral, Dallas, Texas (Orthodox Church in America). Portions were translated by +DMITRI, Archbishop of Dallas and the South. © 2004–2005 St. Seraphim Orthodox Cathedral.
